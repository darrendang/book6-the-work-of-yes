## Canonical finding 1 — a good idea is not yet an earned implementation

> **A good idea is not yet an earned implementation.**

A real need, a promising prototype, employee initiative, existing technology, or enthusiasm can justify further inquiry without yet justifying production commitment.

Support for an objective must not be silently converted into support for every implementation proposed in its name.

## Canonical finding 2 — prototype and production are different decisions

> **A prototype may earn the right to be considered without earning the right to become production.**

A prototype can demonstrate possibility, clarify requirements, create learning, and reveal user value.

Enterprise production introduces additional decision criteria such as architecture, security, privacy, support, ownership, resilience, documentation, governance, technical debt, lifecycle, resource capacity, and opportunity cost.

The closer an experiment moves toward institutional dependence, the more work the yes requires.

## Canonical finding 3 — the easy yes can collapse multiple decisions into one

The source case shows that a single apparent “yes” may conceal several separate choices:

- yes to the business need;
- yes to experimentation;
- yes to employee initiative;
- yes to a prototype as useful evidence;
- yes to a particular production architecture;
- yes to enterprise ownership and maintenance;
- yes to allocating scarce organizational capacity;
- yes to the solution's expected lifecycle and timing.

Responsible judgment may preserve some of these yeses while rejecting, deferring, or reopening others.

## Adopted inquiry

> **What exactly are we saying yes to?**

Use this question when enthusiasm, innovation, urgency, or an already-working prototype risks collapsing distinct commitments into one.

The inquiry should distinguish, where material:

**objective → experiment → prototype → implementation → production architecture → ownership → resource commitment → lifecycle → long-term solution**

This is a decision lens, not locked chapter architecture or a mandatory algorithm.

## Canonical provenance case — visitor-log prototype

A staff-developed visitor-log prototype addressed a legitimate operational need and demonstrated initiative.

Darren's initial stance favored supporting the concept because he wanted to encourage citizen development.

Subsequent review by relevant technology and security roles surfaced material enterprise-readiness work, ongoing support obligations, governance questions, resource tradeoffs, and lifecycle considerations that were not visible in the prototype itself.

The deeper inquiry did not invalidate the business need or the value of citizen development. It changed the decision from an undifferentiated yes into several separate judgments.

## Decision quality, sufficiency, and authority

### Decision quality

The initial positive stance was incomplete because the visible prototype did not expose the full enterprise implications.

The decision improved when additional relevant perspectives surfaced architecture, security, privacy, operations, support, governance, ownership, lifecycle, and opportunity-cost considerations.

### Decision sufficiency

Further inquiry was still materially capable of changing the decision—and did change the understanding of what commitment would require.

The source is therefore a direct counterexample to stopping inquiry at the point where a prototype already “works.”

### Decision authority

Enterprise production implicates responsibilities beyond the citizen developer or sponsoring business unit.

Production ownership, security, support, architecture, organizational risk, portfolio prioritization, and long-term maintenance may belong to different people or governance bodies.

A good local solution does not by itself confer enterprise decision authority.

## Values and tensions

The case should not be reduced to innovation versus control.

Preserve the legitimate tensions:

**empowerment ↔ governance**  
**experimentation ↔ enterprise dependence**  
**speed ↔ sufficient inquiry**  
**local usefulness ↔ organizational opportunity cost**  
**citizen development ↔ institutional ownership**  
**prototype success ↔ production readiness**

Neither side is inherently correct.

## Leadership / perspective boundary

The case supports a Book 6 pattern that relevant expertise can improve a decision before commitment.

The source does not establish that one function or person was simply “right” and another was “wrong.”

The useful pattern is:

**initial perspective → additional relevant perspectives → wider field of consequences → decomposed decision → more earned judgment**
