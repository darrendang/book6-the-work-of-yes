## Existing spine preserved

> **What does someone need to see—or understand—before they have earned enough confidence to decide?**

> **Earn the decision.**

> **The easy no avoids the work of possibility.**
> **The easy yes avoids the work of judgment.**
> **Do not use a decision to avoid the work the decision requires.**

The September 29 refinement makes “enough” and “who should decide?” explicit without privileging yes, no, speed, persistence, or stopping.

## Three distinct dimensions

| Dimension | Approved question |
|---|---|
| Decision quality | Have I seen and understood enough to justify yes or no? |
| Decision sufficiency | When will additional inquiry stop materially improving the choice? |
| Decision authority | Even if I know what I would choose, is the decision actually mine to make? |

These are dimensions of inquiry, not a chapter map or a universal scoring framework.

## Sufficient clarity and warranted confidence

> **Do enough work to gain sufficient clarity and warranted confidence to decide—then decide and move forward.**

> **When is enough, enough?**

Perfect knowledge is not the decision standard. Sufficient inquiry must account for the actual stakes, uncertainty, constraints, time, and consequences.

Too little inquiry can produce premature certainty. Continued inquiry after it ceases to improve judgment can become analysis paralysis or perpetual possibility.

## Make the tradeoffs visible

> **Make the tradeoffs visible.**

Consider material costs and consequences beyond the most visible measure: time, attention, money, organizational capacity, relationships, quality of life, emotional energy, and opportunity cost where they actually matter.

A broader account can change the decision even when no single new fact is decisive. No particular weighting or universal hierarchy is established.

## Conditional stopping rule

> **If additional inquiry is unlikely to materially change the decision, continued inquiry may itself be avoidance.**

“Unlikely” and “may” matter. This is a question for judgment, not permission to stop merely because further work is uncomfortable. Material missing evidence, contrary evidence, unresolved guardrails, or consequences may still justify inquiry or deliberate deferral.

A decision made with sufficient understanding remains open to revision when reality changes. Sufficiency does not make certainty permanent.

## Decision authority and developing judgment

> **Is this decision still mine to make?**

> **Good judgment includes knowing when not to substitute your judgment for someone else's.**

Distinguish:

- **Earning a decision:** doing sufficient work to justify the choice.
- **Earning the ability or delegated responsibility to decide:** developing judgment and receiving an appropriate scope of responsibility.

Ask who owns the consequences, who has decision authority, who is developing judgment, and whether intervention would improve the decision or prevent learning.

Capability and delegated authority are contextual. Intrinsic human agency is not a privilege to be earned from a more experienced person. Knowing a better answer does not by itself confer ownership of another person’s choice.
