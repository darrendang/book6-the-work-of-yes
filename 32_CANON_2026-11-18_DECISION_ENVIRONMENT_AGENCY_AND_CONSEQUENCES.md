## Approved contribution — the environment at decision time

Examine the environment in which a decision is made, alongside the decision-maker's background.

Leadership signals, incentives, resources, accountability, tolerance for error, and consequences can affect which choices appear available, willingness to act, and judgments of what constitutes enough inquiry. This extends the existing situated-knowledge, sufficiency, and authority controls.

Context is a lens across decisions, not a mandatory extra step or a sixth movement.

An environment's preferred speed or risk tolerance does not by itself establish a justified standard. A permissive culture may enable responsible learning or normalize excessive exposure. A cautious culture may protect legitimate interests or discourage warranted action. Preserve both possibilities and investigate the actual decision.

A reported message that failure is acceptable is evidence of the communicated or perceived environment. It is not, by itself, evidence of what happens when a particular decision fails.

## Approved contribution — agency and involvement across contexts

A person's desired involvement can differ across work, family, and other decisions.

Choosing to entrust a decision to another person within understood boundaries can express agency. Distinguish an agreed boundary, a preference to participate, delegated latitude, and actual decision authority.

Do not infer a permanent leader/follower type from a person's choice in one setting. Culture, experience, mentors, and prior expectations may influence which options the chooser recognizes; they are subjects for inquiry, not a deterministic typology.

## Consequences and authority remain separate

Examining a choice includes preparedness for foreseeable consequences and clarity about who bears them.

Willingness to accept personal consequences does not by itself confer authority to impose consequences on others. Conviction, courage, dissent, and acceptance of personal risk do not substitute for evidence, legitimate authority, responsibility, or attention to affected people.

This clarifies an overbroad assistant formulation in the supplied dialogue. Crossing a boundary does not automatically mean someone has earned the right to decide. Preserve the difference between earning confidence in a choice and owning the authority to make it.
