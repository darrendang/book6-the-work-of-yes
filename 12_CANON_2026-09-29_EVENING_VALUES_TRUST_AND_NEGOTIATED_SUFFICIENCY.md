## Governing spine

**What does someone need to see—or understand—before they have earned enough confidence to decide?**

**Earn the decision.**

The easy no avoids the work of possibility. The easy yes avoids the work of judgment. Do not use a decision to avoid the work the decision requires.

Decision quality, sufficiency, and authority remain distinct. These refinements explain how criteria, perspective, relationships, and negotiation bear on them.

## Values and criteria give sufficiency meaning

Enough must be considered in relation to what the decision is meant to serve. Identify material values, priorities, desired benefits, acceptable costs, consequences, and genuine constraints.

Evidence informs whether an alternative meets those criteria; more evidence does not by itself determine which priorities should govern. Preference alone does not settle responsibilities to other affected people.

Adopted inquiry:

> **Enough for what, and according to whose priorities?**

Criteria may be revised through learning. Preserve why they changed rather than silently rewriting them to justify a preferred answer.

## Widening and narrowing serve different functions

Widen perspective to discover relevant alternatives, overlooked values, assumptions, and consequences. Focus the choice by judging what is material.

Narrowing is warranted by relevance and sufficient understanding, not by a desire to remove inconvenient evidence. Ask what justifies excluding an alternative and whether including it could materially change the choice.

This conceptual distinction is adopted. A prescribed widen-then-narrow sequence, technique set, or decision algorithm remains a method hypothesis. Inquiry may be recursive.

## Grounded sufficiency

Grounded sufficiency names enough understanding to justify the particular choice or next step while acknowledging remaining uncertainty. It develops the existing standard of sufficient clarity and warranted confidence.

A viable path and meaningful guardrails may justify a bounded next step; they do not establish eventual success, eliminate uncertainty, or justify every later commitment. The evidence required must fit the step's consequences and reversibility.

Do not use grounded sufficiency to rename impatience or avoid required inquiry.

## Reciprocal trust

Trust in decisions and delegated responsibility runs in both directions. Examine what the person receiving responsibility must be able to rely on from the person granting it, as well as the judgment expected from the recipient.

Trust remains contextual. Competence in one domain does not prove competence in all domains; trust does not itself grant authority over another person's choice or remove actual oversight responsibilities.

The conceptual reciprocity is adopted. Consistency, clear boundaries, responses to mistakes, and other proposed components remain hypotheses requiring stories and counterexamples. No parenting, employment, or board-governance formula is established.

## Negotiated sufficiency

Interest-based bargaining requires examining each party's goals, interests, objectives, and thresholds. One party's definition of enough does not by itself establish a satisfactory agreement.

Learning and compromise may justify revisiting a proposed minimum. Distinguish an informed revision from conceding too much merely to obtain agreement. Preserve the new understanding, tradeoff, and authority supporting a revised threshold.

A negotiable preference is not the same as an actual guardrail. Required legal, ethical, safety, fiduciary, or other genuine constraints are not waived by agreement or convenience.

Agreement is not always the responsible outcome. Earned refusal or justified deferral remains possible.
