## Existing spine preserved

> **What does someone need to see—or understand—before they have earned enough confidence to decide?**

> **Earn the decision.**

> **Do not use a decision to avoid the work the decision requires.**

Decision quality, sufficiency, and authority remain distinct. This record adds an outcome-quality and handoff dimension without replacing the existing spine.

## Canonical finding 1 — Decision quality is not outcome quality

> **An earned decision does not guarantee a good outcome.**

A decision should be judged by the information, criteria, tradeoffs, authority, uncertainty, and consequences reasonably visible when the choice was made.

A disappointing later outcome does not by itself prove the earlier decision was poorly reasoned.

A favorable later outcome does not by itself prove the earlier decision was well reasoned; weak judgment can be rewarded by luck.

Outcomes still matter. They become evidence for learning and revision. They must not be used to silently rewrite what the decision-maker knew at the time.

## Canonical finding 2 — A good decision may change the risk rather than eliminate it

The LAUP handoff exposed a legitimate tension:

**conflict-free transfer / successor autonomy ↔ continuity / fidelity to the original decision rationale**

The decision to transfer implementation authority reduced a governance and conflict-of-interest risk while accepting continuity and interpretation risk.

Responsible decision-making does not always remove uncertainty. Sometimes the work is to identify which risks should be carried, by whom, and why.

## Canonical finding 3 — Genuine transfer of authority permits different judgment

> **A genuine transfer of decision authority includes the possibility that the successor will decide differently.**

Decision authority is not authentic if the prior decision-maker retains enough control to guarantee the successor's answer.

This does not imply that continuity, guardrails, accountability, or institutional memory are unimportant. It creates the opposite question: what understanding must survive the handoff even when control of the decision does not?

## Canonical handoff inquiry

> **What understanding must survive a handoff even when the authority to decide changes hands?**

A successor may need access to the rationale, criteria, constraints, stakeholder learning, tradeoffs, evidence, and unresolved uncertainty that made the inherited direction intelligible.

Book 6 does not yet prescribe a universal handoff method. Possible mechanisms remain development hypotheses until tested against additional cases.

## Canonical LAUP provenance case

LAUP is admitted as a canonical Book 6 provenance case for the distinction between decision quality and outcome quality.

Publicly corroborated facts include:

- the February 12, 2004 Universal Preschool Master Plan for Los Angeles County;
- a broad collaborative planning process involving representatives across sectors;
- Karen Hill-Scott & Company as lead consultant to First 5 LA;
- LAUP's April 2005 statement that the process drew more than 600 parents, providers, and community leaders;
- Darren Dang's listing on the consulting team in the Master Plan;

Darren's firsthand source statement adds:

- he and Karen deliberately did not seek to run the implementation organization because they wanted to avoid the conflict or appearance of conflict created by designing the plan and then controlling its execution;
- he experienced the later trajectory as a disappointing departure from important parts of the comprehensive community-developed vision.

The latter interpretation remains attributed firsthand recollection unless independently corroborated.

## Outcome boundary

Do not reduce the case to “LAUP failed” or “the Master Plan failed.”

Public evidence shows substantial implementation and later scale. The Book 6 case concerns a narrower decision problem:

**an ethically motivated, conflict-free transfer of authority that Darren judges to have been well reasoned at the time, followed by a downstream trajectory he experienced as disappointing.**

The case is valuable precisely because meaningful public impact and disappointment with fidelity to the original vision can both be true.

## Hindsight safeguard

Do not infer from the later outcome that Darren and Karen should have retained operational control.

Also do not infer that the handoff was necessarily optimal merely because the conflict-of-interest rationale was principled.

The development question remains:

> **Did we make the wrong decision—or did we make a sound decision while accepting a risk that later materialized?**

A related open question is whether the handoff itself could have transferred more context or institutional memory without undermining genuine successor authority. Do not answer that from hindsight without contemporaneous evidence.
