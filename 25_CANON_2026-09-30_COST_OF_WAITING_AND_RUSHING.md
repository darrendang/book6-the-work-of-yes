## Existing spine preserved

> **What does someone need to see—or understand—before they have earned enough confidence to decide?**

> **Earn the decision.**

Decision sufficiency remains contextual rather than formulaic. Existing controls already require consideration of stakes, uncertainty, constraints, time, consequences, values, priorities, and whether additional inquiry is likely to materially improve the choice.

This refinement makes one two-sided pressure explicit.

## Canonical refinement

> **Consider both the cost of waiting and the cost of rushing.**

Indecision is not neutral. Delay can consume time, money, attention, credibility, momentum, opportunity, or other resources relevant to the decision.

Speed is not neutral either. Acting too quickly can create avoidable commitments, foreclose alternatives, increase downside exposure, or lock in consequences before enough has been understood.

Neither cost automatically governs. The work is to understand whether waiting longer is likely to improve the decision enough to justify what waiting costs, and whether acting sooner is worth the risks created by less inquiry.

## Relationship to sufficiency

This refinement develops the existing question:

> **When is enough, enough?**

A decision does not require perfect information. Nor does the existence of a deadline justify premature closure.

Decision sufficiency therefore includes judgment about the marginal value of more inquiry relative to the consequences of delay and the consequences of acting too soon.

Adopted development inquiry:

> **What is the cost of waiting—and what is the cost of rushing?**

This is a decision tension, not a universal equation, score, or algorithm.

## Guardrails

- Do not use “cost of waiting” to rationalize impatience when material evidence is still missing.
- Do not use “cost of rushing” to rationalize endless inquiry after additional work is unlikely to change the decision.
- Costs may be financial, operational, relational, personal, strategic, temporal, or opportunity-based when they are genuinely material.
- The weighting of those costs depends on the actual decision, stakeholders, authority, reversibility, and consequences.
- Sufficiency remains open to revision when circumstances or evidence change.
